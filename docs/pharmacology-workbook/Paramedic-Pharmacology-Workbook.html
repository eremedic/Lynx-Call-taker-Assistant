Chapter one

Medication safety foundations

Drug math only matters if it lands in the right patient, by the right route, at the right time. Most medication errors in EMS are not arithmetic failures. They come from grabbing the wrong vial, misreading a concentration, or a decimal point in the wrong place. Build the habits in this chapter before you build speed.

The rights of medication administration

  1. Right patient: confirm identity and allergies.
  2. Right medication: read the label three times (when you pick it up, draw it up, and give it).
  3. Right dose: calculate, then have a partner verify.
  4. Right route: IV, IO, IM, IN, SL, PO, neb, ET.
  5. Right time: rate of push, interval between doses.
  6. Right documentation: drug, dose, route, time, response.
  7. Right reason, response, and right to refuse.

High-alert medications

Vasopressors (norepinephrine, epinephrine infusions, dopamine), opioids, sedatives, insulin, potassium and concentrated electrolytes cause serious harm in small errors. Use an independent double-check: your partner calculates the dose separately before you compare answers. Reading your answer aloud for them to agree with does not count as a check.

Field tip: does the answer make sense?

Before you push, ask whether the number is plausible. Most single IV push doses fit in 0.1 to 10 mL. If your answer is 25 mL of fentanyl or 0.02 mL of naloxone, stop and recalculate.

The metric system and conversions

Almost every drug calculation starts with getting the order and the vial into the same unit. If the order is in micrograms and the vial is labeled in milligrams, convert first. The metric system makes this easy: each common step is a factor of 1,000.

The decimal-shift method

Multiplying or dividing by 1,000 means moving the decimal point three places. Move it right when going to a smaller unit, left when going to a larger unit.

IV fluids in the paramedic scope

IV fluids are drugs. Each one has indications, contraindications and a dose. To choose well you need two ideas: where water lives in the body, and tonicity, which decides which way water moves once a fluid is in the bloodstream.

Body fluid compartments

About 60% of an adult's body weight is water. Two-thirds of it sits inside cells (intracellular fluid, ICF). The remaining third is extracellular (ECF), split between the interstitial space around the cells and the plasma inside the vessels.

Tonicity: which way does water move?

Tonicity compares a fluid's effective solute concentration to plasma (about 275–295 mOsm/kg). Water always moves toward the side with more solute.

The fluids you will hang

Most paramedic formularies carry normal saline and lactated Ringer's for volume, D5W as a drug diluent, and D10 for hypoglycemia. Some services also carry a balanced solution or monitor hypotonic maintenance fluids on interfacility transfers. Bags come in 100, 250, 500 and 1,000 mL sizes.

Colloids and blood

Colloids (albumin, hetastarch) contain large molecules that stay in the vessels. Blood products (packed red cells, plasma, whole blood) are the preferred replacement for hemorrhage. Most paramedics only monitor these during transfers; some critical care and prehospital blood programs start them under specific protocols.

Catheters and administration sets

Flow through a catheter depends mostly on its diameter and length (Poiseuille's law). Doubling the radius raises flow sixteen-fold, so a short, large-bore catheter is what you want for volume. Gauge numbers run backwards: the smaller the number, the bigger the catheter.

Dose and volume: how much to draw up

The order tells you the dose (mg, mcg, g, units). The vial tells you the concentration: how much drug is in each mL. Your job is to find the volume that holds the ordered dose.

Desired over haveFormula 4.1
Volume to give (mL) = DH × Q
D desired dose (the order) H dose on hand (drug amount on the label) Q quantity (the volume that holds H)

D and H must be in the same unit. Convert first if they are not.

Worked example 4.1

Fentanyl 75 mcg IV. Vial: 100 mcg / 2 mL

Identify
D = 75 mcg · H = 100 mcg · Q = 2 mL
Units
Both in mcg. No conversion needed.
Solve
75 ÷ 100 × 2 = 0.75 × 2 = 1.5 mL
Check
75 is a bit less than 100, so the answer should be a bit less than 2 mL. It is.
Answer
Draw up 1.5 mL

Weight-based calculations

Children, and many adult drugs, are dosed per kilogram. The order reads like mg/kg or mcg/kg. This adds one step in front of the dose-volume formula: find the patient's dose from their weight.

Drip rates I: volume over time

Volume-based drip problems give you a volume of fluid and a time to run it over. Without a pump, you control the rate by counting drops per minute in the drip chamber. With a pump, you enter mL/hr.

Gravity drip rateFormula 6.1
gtt/min =volume (mL) × drop factor (gtt/mL)time (min)
mL/hr =volume (mL)time (hr)
drop factor from the tubing package: 10, 15, 20 or 60time in minutes for gtt/min
Worked example 6.1

Infuse 250 mL NS over 30 minutes with a 10 gtt/mL set

Set up
(250 mL × 10 gtt/mL) ÷ 30 min
Solve
2,500 ÷ 30 = 83.3
Answer
83 gtt/min ≈ 21 drops every 15 seconds

Pump rate

Field tip: counting drops

Count drops for 15 seconds and multiply by 4. To set 83 gtt/min, aim for about 21 drops in 15 seconds. Recheck after moving the patient: bag height, arm position and kinks all change a gravity rate.

Is the rate countable?

Above about 100–150 gtt/min the drops blur into a stream and can't be counted. If your answer is 300 gtt/min, switch to a set with a smaller drop factor, use a pump, or run it wide open with a pressure bag as protocol allows.

Drip rates II: concentration infusions

A medication infusion is ordered as a dose over time, such as mcg/min, mg/min or mcg/kg/min. To run it you need to know how much drug is in each mL of the bag (the concentration), then turn the dose per minute into mL per minute.

Concentration infusionFormula 7.1
mL/hr =dose per min × 60concentration (per mL)
gtt/min =dose per min × drop factorconcentration (per mL)
Weight-based: dose per min = (dose per kg per min) × kg
Worked example 7.1

Norepinephrine 8 mcg/min. Bag: 4 mg in 250 mL. 60 gtt/mL set or pump.

Convert
4 mg = 4,000 mcg (order is in mcg)
Conc.
4,000 mcg ÷ 250 mL = 16 mcg/mL
mL/min
8 mcg/min ÷ 16 mcg/mL = 0.5 mL/min
Pump
0.5 × 60 = 30 mL/hr
Gravity
0.5 × 60 gtt/mL = 30 gtt/min
Answer
30 mL/hr = 30 gtt/min on a microdrip
Worked example 7.2

Dopamine 5 mcg/kg/min for an 80 kg patient. Premix: 400 mg in 250 mL.

Dose/min
5 mcg/kg/min × 80 kg = 400 mcg/min
Conc.
400,000 mcg ÷ 250 mL = 1,600 mcg/mL
mL/min
400 ÷ 1,600 = 0.25 mL/min
Pump
0.25 × 60 = 15 mL/hr
Answer
15 mL/hr = 15 gtt/min on a microdrip
Worked example 7.3

Timed infusion: amiodarone 150 mg in 100 mL D5W over 10 minutes, 10 gtt/mL set

Idea
The whole bag runs in 10 minutes, so this is really a volume-over-time problem.
Gravity
(100 mL × 10) ÷ 10 min = 100 gtt/min
Pump
100 mL ÷ (10/60 hr) = 600 mL/hr, VTBI 100 mL
Answer
100 gtt/min or 600 mL/hr

Working backward: what dose is running?

On a transfer you often inherit a running drip. Reverse the formula to confirm the dose matches the order.

Dose from rateFormula 7.2
dose per min =mL/hr × concentration60

Example: norepinephrine 16 mcg/mL running at 15 mL/hr → 15 × 16 ÷ 60 = 4 mcg/min.

Calcium chloride vs. calcium gluconate

Both are 10%, but calcium chloride delivers about three times more elemental calcium per mL. They are not interchangeable mL for mL. Confirm which one the protocol names.

Why ratio labels changed

Since 2016 US labels state epinephrine as mg/mL (1 mg/mL, 0.1 mg/mL) instead of 1:1,000 and 1:10,000, because ratio labels caused 10-fold errors. You will still hear the old names on calls and see them in older references.

IV infusion pumps

A volumetric infusion pump pushes fluid at a set rate in mL/hr, regardless of bag height, back-pressure or patient position. That makes it the standard for medication infusions, children, and any transfer where the rate must be exact. Pumps differ by brand, but the controls and concepts below are common to all of them. Learn your service's model from its manufacturer training.

#PartWhat it does
1DisplayShows drug, concentration, dose, rate (mL/hr), VTBI, volume infused, alarms and battery.
2Soft keysContext buttons whose labels change on screen: dose, VTBI, options, secondary.
3Numeric keypadEnters rate, dose, weight and volume. Watch the decimal key.
4Start · Pause · Channel offStart runs the program. Pause holds it. Channel off stops it and clears that channel.
5Door and setThe pump segment of the tubing loads here. The pumping mechanism (peristaltic fingers or a cassette) moves fluid. Anti-free-flow clamps close when the door opens.
6SensorsUpstream and downstream pressure sensors detect occlusions; the air-in-line detector catches bubbles.
7Power / batteryShows AC or battery operation and charge left. Plan for transport time.
8Pole clampMounts the pump to an IV pole or stretcher mount.

Key terms

RateVolume per hour, in mL/hr. The pump always runs on this, even in dose mode.
VTBIVolume to be infused. The pump stops (or drops to KVO) when it reaches this. Set it to the volume in the bag or the volume ordered, whichever is less.
Volume infusedRunning total. Record it at handoff and clear it at the start of your transport.
Rate mode vs. dose modeRate mode: you enter mL/hr. Dose mode: you enter drug, concentration, weight and dose, and the pump calculates mL/hr. Verify the calculation yourself.
Drug library (DERS)Dose error reduction software: preloaded drugs, standard concentrations and dose limits by care area. Always choose the drug from the library instead of using basic rate mode.
Soft limitAn unusual dose. The pump warns you; you can override after re-checking.
Hard limitAn unsafe dose. The pump will not accept it. Re-check the order and the math.
KVOKeep vein open: a low rate (often 1–5 mL/hr) the pump falls back to when VTBI is complete.
Primary vs. secondaryPrimary is the main infusion. Secondary (piggyback) runs a short infusion through the same channel, then returns to primary.
Bolus / loading doseA set amount delivered over a set time from the same bag, on pumps that support it.

Programming a medication infusion, step by step

  1. Verify the order and the rights. Drug, concentration, dose, patient weight, route, and any titration parameters.
  2. Calculate the expected rate by hand. You need your own number to check the pump against (Chapter 7).
  3. Spike and prime the set. Clear all air, label the tubing with the drug name, and close the roller clamp.
  4. Power on and choose the care area or profile. This sets which drug library and limits apply.
  5. Load the set into the channel and close the door. Orient the set in the direction of flow (bag above, patient below).
  6. Select the drug from the library. Confirm the concentration on screen matches the bag label exactly.
  7. Enter weight (if weight-based), dose and VTBI. Read the rate the pump calculated.
  8. Compare the pump's rate to your hand calculation. If they differ, stop and find out why before you start.
  9. Independent double-check with your partner. They check drug, concentration, dose, rate, line and patient on their own.
  10. Open the roller clamp, connect to the patient, press Start. Trace the line from bag to patient to confirm the right channel and port.
  11. Monitor. Watch the drip chamber, vital signs and IV site. Document start time, dose and rate.

Running a secondary (piggyback) infusion

  1. Hang the secondary bag higher than the primary; use the extension hook to lower the primary.
  2. Connect the secondary line to the upper Y-site, above the pump.
  3. Select Secondary on the pump. Enter the drug, rate and VTBI for the secondary.
  4. Open the secondary clamp. Watch the secondary drip chamber to confirm it is the one dripping.
  5. When VTBI is complete the pump returns to the primary rate. Confirm the primary resumed.
Common miss

If the secondary clamp is closed, or the bags are at the same height, the pump runs primary fluid at the secondary rate. Always look at which chamber is dripping.

Pumps on interfacility transports

  • Verify on arrival. Match each channel against the transfer orders: drug, concentration, dose, rate, line and port. Record volume infused, then clear it.
  • Count the bags. Will each bag and the battery last the trip plus delays? Use Formula 6.2.
  • Move one thing at a time. When switching from the hospital's pump to yours, clamp the line before opening any door so nothing free-flows, reprogram, re-verify, then unclamp.
  • Stay in scope. Only run and titrate medications your protocols and the transfer orders allow.
  • Hand off by reading back. At the receiving facility, read each pump's settings aloud with the receiving clinician.
